Healthcare recruiting glossary.

Plain-English definitions of the credentials, training stages and terms you meet every day in clinical recruiting.

Provider types and credentials

MD, Doctor of Medicine
A physician trained at an allopathic medical school. The most common medical degree in the United States, equal in scope of practice to a DO.
DO, Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine
A physician trained at an osteopathic medical school. DOs and MDs are licensed the same way in all 50 states and practice every specialty. Training adds osteopathic manipulative treatment.
NP, Nurse Practitioner
An advanced practice registered nurse with an MSN or DNP who can diagnose, treat and prescribe. The level of physician oversight depends on state law.
PA, Physician Assistant (Physician Associate)
A clinician with a master's-level PA degree, licensed to practice medicine in collaboration with a physician. PAs can prescribe in all 50 states.
RN, Registered Nurse
A nurse with an ADN or BSN who has passed the NCLEX-RN and holds a state license.
APRN, Advanced Practice Registered Nurse
The umbrella term for NPs, CRNAs, certified nurse-midwives and clinical nurse specialists.
CRNA, Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetist
An APRN who specializes in anesthesia. One of the highest-paid nursing roles and often in short supply.
SRNA, Student Registered Nurse Anesthetist
A nurse in a nurse anesthesia program, usually 12 to 36 months from practice and about to make a first practice decision.
LPN or LVN, Licensed Practical or Vocational Nurse
A nurse with a one-year diploma or certificate who works under an RN or physician.
Allied health
Clinical professionals who are not physicians or nurses: physical, occupational and respiratory therapists, radiology techs, surgical techs, sonographers and others.

Training stages

Medical student
A student in an MD or DO program, usually four years.
PGY, Post-Graduate Year
The year of training after medical school. PGY-1 is intern year. Family medicine residency runs PGY-1 to PGY-3; neurosurgery runs to PGY-7. Oslr reads the PGY year from when residency started.
Resident
A physician in specialty training after medical school. Length depends on the specialty: three years for family medicine, five for general surgery and orthopedic surgery, seven for neurosurgery.
Chief resident
A senior resident with teaching and administrative duties, usually in the final year.
Fellow
A physician who has finished residency and is training in a subspecialty. A cardiology fellow has already completed internal medicine residency.
Attending
A fully trained physician practicing independently after residency and any fellowship.
The Match (NRMP)
The annual process that pairs medical students with U.S. residency programs. Match Day is in March and residencies start in late June or early July. Most fellowship matches run in the fall and winter.

Identifiers and credentialing

NPI, National Provider Identifier
A unique 10-digit number CMS assigns to U.S. healthcare providers for billing and electronic transactions. NPI records are public but are often out of date on practice location and employer.
DEA number
A registration with the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration that allows a provider to prescribe controlled substances.
State medical license
A state-issued license to practice medicine. Many physicians hold licenses in several states. Verification runs through state medical boards.
Board certification
A voluntary credential showing a physician met a specialty board's standards, such as the American Board of Internal Medicine.
Board eligible
A physician who has finished an accredited residency and can sit for the board exam but has not yet passed it.
ABMS, American Board of Medical Specialties
The umbrella organization for the 24 specialty certifying boards.
CME, Continuing Medical Education
Ongoing education physicians complete to keep licensure and board certification.
Credentialing
How a hospital or health system verifies training, licensure and qualifications before granting privileges. Recruiters need to know how long it takes to set realistic start dates.
Privileging
A hospital's permission for a clinician to perform specific procedures or services there.

International medical graduates

IMG, International Medical Graduate
A physician who attended medical school outside the U.S. or Canada. IMGs make up roughly a quarter of U.S. physicians.
USMLE, United States Medical Licensing Examination
The three-step exam every physician must pass to be licensed in the U.S.
ECFMG, Educational Commission for Foreign Medical Graduates
The body that certifies IMGs as eligible to enter U.S. residency.
J-1 and H-1B
Visa categories IMGs commonly work under. J-1 visas often require a return home that can be waived, for example by working in a Health Professional Shortage Area.

Practice settings

Hospitalist
A physician, usually internal or family medicine trained, who practices only in the hospital and manages inpatients.
Locum tenens
Latin for "holding the place of." A temporary physician or APRN role, often days to months, usually paid through a staffing firm.
Per diem
Work scheduled day by day, common for nurses and allied health.
Academic medicine
Practice at a university or teaching hospital, combining patient care, teaching and research.
Private practice
A physician-owned or small group practice, often single-specialty.
ASC, Ambulatory Surgery Center
A facility for same-day surgery, separate from a hospital.
FQHC, Federally Qualified Health Center
A community clinic that receives federal funding to serve underserved patients. FQHC roles often qualify for loan repayment.

Compensation and contracts

W-2 and 1099
Whether a clinician is an employee (W-2) or an independent contractor (1099). Locums are usually 1099; hospital-employed roles are usually W-2.
RVU, Relative Value Unit
The unit CMS uses to value physician services. Many pay models pay a set amount per RVU.
Compensation model
How a physician is paid: salary, productivity, salary plus bonus, base plus RVU, or a partnership track in private practice.
Sign-on bonus
A one-time payment to a new hire, often tied to a multi-year commitment.
Loan repayment
Federal, state or employer programs that pay down medical school debt in exchange for service.

Recruiting and sourcing

Sourcing
Finding and identifying potential candidates. Separate from recruiting, which moves them through hiring, and credentialing, which verifies them after an offer.
Pipeline
The candidates a recruiter is tracking against open or future roles.
Passive candidate
Someone who is not job hunting but might consider the right role. Most fellowship-trained physicians are passive.
ATS, Applicant Tracking System
Software for job postings, applications and hiring workflow, such as Workday, Greenhouse, Bullhorn or iCIMS.
CRM, Candidate Relationship Management
Software recruiters use to track outreach and long-term candidate relationships.
ICP, Ideal Candidate Profile
A description of the candidate a search targets. In healthcare it usually covers specialty, training stage, board status, location and practice setting.
Sequence
A series of outreach emails sent over time, usually paused when the candidate replies.

Regulatory and compliance

HIPAA
The federal law on the privacy and security of Protected Health Information. It applies to covered entities and their business associates. Recruiting platforms that do not handle PHI are generally outside its scope.
PHI, Protected Health Information
Identifiable patient health information such as records, diagnoses, prescriptions and lab results. A clinician's specialty, training and work contact details are not PHI.
CMS, Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services
The federal agency that runs Medicare and Medicaid and maintains the NPI Registry.
CAN-SPAM
The U.S. law on commercial email: accurate sender details, a clear unsubscribe, a postal address and honest subject lines. Recruiting outreach generally falls under it.
