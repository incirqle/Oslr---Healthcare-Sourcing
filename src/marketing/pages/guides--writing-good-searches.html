Writing good searches

How to phrase a search so Oslr returns the right clinicians the first time.

  4. Be specific about geography
  5. Use the clinical term

  7. Check what Oslr understood

  9. When a search needs more

Oslr takes plain English. Some phrasings still work better than others. This is the short version of what to do, what to skip, and why.

Start with the role

Searches that lead with a clinical role (physician, surgeon, resident, NP, PA, RN, CRNA) work better than searches that describe the person.

  • Better: Family medicine physicians in the Mountain West
  • Weaker: Doctors who like rural medicine

Oslr reads the clinical role first. When the role is buried under modifiers, results get noisier.

One specialty per search

Combining several specialties in one search returns a mixed list. Run them separately and compare.

  • Better: Interventional cardiologists in Florida, then a separate search for Electrophysiologists in Florida
  • Weaker: Interventional cardiologists, electrophysiologists and structural heart cardiologists in Florida

Separate searches also show you how much supply each subspecialty really has in your market.

Name the training stage you mean

Training stage is where Oslr does the most work for you. It reads when residency or fellowship started, so the year in your search is checked against real dates, not just the title.

  • PGY-5 orthopedic residents in New York City
  • Current cardiology fellows in Texas
  • Final-year family medicine residents in Ohio

A resident's title stays the same for every year of training. The start date is what separates a PGY-2 from a PGY-5.

Be specific about geography

Oslr handles cities, metros, states and named regions such as the Southeast or the Mountain West. It does not guess from loose phrasing.

  • Better: Hospitalists in Texas, Oklahoma or Louisiana
  • Weaker: Hospitalists in the south central area

Use the clinical term

Oslr knows healthcare vocabulary, so use it.

  • Better: Nurse anesthetists in Atlanta
  • Weaker: People who put patients to sleep before surgery in Atlanta

If you are not sure of a term, the healthcare recruiting glossary covers the common ones.

Start broad, then refine

A search that stacks every requirement at once returns very few people. Start with role, specialty and location, then narrow with Refine and your criteria.

Check what Oslr understood

After every search, Oslr shows the filters it read from your words. If it picked up something you did not mean, fix it before you scroll:

  • Click Refine to edit the filters
  • Remove any filter chip you do not want
  • Run New Search to start again with different phrasing

A quick look at the parsed filters saves a long scroll through the wrong people.

Health systems include their affiliates

A search for a health system includes its affiliated facilities. A Mayo Clinic search covers Mayo's other locations. For one facility, add the city (Mayo Clinic in Rochester) or a more specific entity name.

When a search needs more

Two cases ask you to add detail:

  • No clinical intent. People in Nashville has no role, specialty or employer. Cardiologists in Nashville does.
  • Too broad. A search that would return thousands of people with no specific role or employer. Add a specialty, title, location or employer.

Things you can skip

  • Boolean strings. cardiologist AND interventional NOT structural does not help. Write it the way you would say it.
  • Quotation marks. They do not change the search.
  • License status. Oslr does not filter on license status today, so those words are ignored.

Three patterns that work

  1. Role + specialty + location: Cardiologists in Houston, ICU nurses in Atlanta
  2. Role + employer: Spine surgeons at Mayo Clinic, Hospitalists at HCA Healthcare
  3. Training stage + specialty + location: PGY-5 orthopedic residents in New York City, Current GI fellows in Texas

These cover most of the searches recruiters run every day.
